# Patterns to remove

Source: Wikipedia, "Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing), checked against the live article in October 2026. Kirsty reviewed the list. Each entry says what the pattern is and what to do instead.

Two caveats from the article itself. First, none of these alone proves a text is AI-written, and perfect grammar, formal prose, or a plain tone aren't signs. Second, AI habits change over time: newer models dropped some old tells (em dashes, "delve") and picked up new ones (items 10, 11 and 24). The goal isn't to make text messy. It's to make it sound like a person.

## Content

1. **Inflated significance or legacy.** "Stands as a testament/reminder," "plays a crucial/pivotal/vital/key role," "a key turning point," "marks a shift," "reflects broader trends," "symbolizing its enduring...," "setting the stage for," "shaping the," "focal point," "evolving landscape," "indelible mark," "deeply rooted," "this distinction matters because." Instead, state the concrete fact and let the reader judge how important it is.
2. **Canned notability and media coverage.** "Independent coverage," "regional/national media outlets," "trade publications," "has been featured/profiled/cited in," "written by a leading expert," "was identified by," "maintains an active social media presence." Instead, name the outlet and what it actually said, or cut it.
3. **Tacked-on "-ing" analysis.** Sentences ending in "highlighting/underscoring/emphasizing...," "ensuring...," "reflecting/symbolizing...," "contributing to...," "cultivating/fostering...," "encompassing...," "enhancing...," or phrases like "valuable insights," "resonates with." Instead, end the sentence at the fact, or give the effect its own sentence with a real subject. Never pin an analysis on a named source unless that source said it.
4. **Promotional puffery.** "Boasts a," "vibrant," "rich," "profound," "exemplifies," "commitment to," "natural beauty," "nestled," "in the heart of," "groundbreaking," "renowned," "diverse array," "featuring," "world-class." Newer models are subtler about this, so watch for quietly glowing tone too. Instead, describe what's there.
5. **Vague connection.** "Associated with," "connected to," "in connection with," "in association with," used instead of saying the actual relationship. Instead, say it: "was CEO of," "taught at," "funded."
6. **Vague attribution (weasel words).** "Experts argue," "Observers have cited," "Industry reports suggest," "Some critics say," "several sources" when there's one, "such as" in front of what's really the whole list. Instead, name the source, give the real count, or drop the claim.
7. **Formula "challenges and future prospects" ending.** "Despite its successes, X faces several challenges...," "Despite these challenges...," then a hopeful line or speculation about future initiatives. Sections called "Challenges and Legacy" or "Future Outlook." Instead, name the real challenge and what's being done, or leave it out. (Mentioning challenges is fine. The formula is the problem.)
8. **"X and Y" headings, especially "Awards and recognition."** Instead, use a plain heading that fits the content, or no heading.
9. **Defining a topic as if it were a thing.** Opening with "X refers to..." or treating a broad title ("Affordable housing in Prince George's County") as a proper noun. Instead, start with the subject itself.

## Word choice and grammar

10. **AI vocabulary.** The article tracks this by era, and all of it should go:
    - Older (2023 to 2024): additionally (especially to open a sentence), boasts, bolstered, crucial, delve, emphasizing, enduring, garner, intricate/intricacies, interplay, key (as an adjective), landscape (abstract), meticulously, pivotal, underscore, tapestry, testament, valuable, vibrant, deep dive.
    - Mid-2024 to mid-2025: align with, enhance, fostering, highlighting, showcasing.
    - Mid-2025 to mid-2026: emphasizing, enhance, highlighting, showcasing.
    - Newest (mid-2026 on): challenge, clearer, dependable, echoed, foster, leverage, matters, multifaceted, practical, prioritize, quietly, steady, universally.
    - Also watch (common in business writing from AI): seamless, robust, holistic, empower, navigate (abstract), realm, transformative, game-changer, unlock, elevate, streamline, synergy.
    - Instead, use the plain word: also, important, help, show, use, has. Some of these words have ordinary meanings ("a steady job," "a practical problem"). The issue is the abstract, filler use.
11. **Avoiding "is" and "has."** "Serves as," "stands as," "marks," "functions as," "operates as," "represents," "boasts," "features," "maintains," "offers," "refers to," where "is" or "has" works. Instead, use "is," "are," "has."
12. **Negative parallelisms.** All three forms: "Not only X, but also Y" / "It's not just X, it's Y"; "It's not X, it's Y" / "No X, no Y, just Z"; and the reversed "Y rather than X," which is especially common in newer models. Instead, state Y directly.
13. **Rule of three.** Three adjectives, three short phrases, three benefits, used to make a thin point look complete. Instead, use the number of items you really have.
14. **False ranges.** "From X to Y" where X and Y don't sit on a real scale ("from grassroots advocacy to cutting-edge technology"). Instead, list the actual things or pick one.
15. **Elegant variation.** Swapping synonyms for the same thing (the organization, the group, the entity, the firm). Instead, pick one name and repeat it.
16. **Stiff synonyms.** "Authored," "relocated," "utilized," "attempted," "passed away." Instead: wrote, moved, used, tried, died.

## Rhythm and punctuation

17. **Em dashes (—).** Still a strong tell for Claude specifically, and often spaced ( — ). Instead, use a comma, colon, parentheses, or a new sentence. Don't swap in a spaced hyphen ( - ) as a disguised dash.
18. **Flat punctuation and same-length sentences.** New in 2026: models that stopped using em dashes now lean on only commas and periods, with every sentence 8 to 20 words long. That uniformity is now its own tell. Instead, mix it up: a short sentence, then a long one; a colon or semicolon where it fits; parentheses for an aside; the occasional question.
19. **Curly quotes and apostrophes** mixed with straight ones. Instead, use straight ' and " consistently. (Kirsty's Word or iPhone may auto-curl quotes. That's fine if it's consistent.)
20. **Hyphens for ranges.** "2019-2024." Instead, en dash (2019–2024) or "2019 to 2024."

## Formatting

21. **Title Case In Headings.** Instead, sentence case.
22. **Too much bold**, especially bolding the same key phrase every time it shows up. Instead, almost none.
23. **Bold-label bullets.** List items that open with a bold label and a colon ("**Scalability:** ..."), including ones using •, – or emoji as bullets. Instead, write a sentence, or plain bullets without labels. (This file uses them only because it's an internal checklist.)
24. **Heading mistakes.** A heading that repeats the document's title at the top, skipped heading levels, headings with nothing under them except more headings. Instead, only the headings the reader needs.
25. **Emoji** in headings or bullets. Instead, none, unless Kirsty's original used them on purpose.
26. **Small, unnecessary tables.** Instead, a sentence, unless the data really is a grid.
27. **Divider lines (---) between every section.** Instead, let headings do the work.
28. **Letter or email framing where it doesn't belong.** Salutations, subject lines, sign-offs on something that isn't a letter. Instead, start with the content.

## Chatbot leftovers

29. **Helper lines.** "I hope this helps," "Certainly!", "Of course!", "You're absolutely right!", "Would you like...", "Is there anything else," "Let me know," "Here is a...," "a more detailed breakdown." Instead, delete. If an email needs a closing line, make it specific ("Can you send the budget by Friday?").
30. **Hedging about sources.** "While specific details are limited," "not widely documented/disclosed," "in the available sources," "based on available information," "should be treated as X rather than Y," "does not by itself establish." Newer models do this a lot. Instead, say what's known plainly. If something is missing, flag it for Kirsty rather than writing around it.
31. **Knowledge-cutoff lines.** "As of my last update," "Up to my last training update." Instead, delete, or use the actual date.
32. **Lecturing asides.** "It's important/crucial to note," "It's worth noting," "may vary." Instead, just say the thing.
33. **Summary wrap-ups.** "In summary," "In conclusion," "Overall," or a "Conclusion" section that restates. Instead, end on the last real point.
34. **AI self-reference or refusals.** "As an AI language model..." Instead, delete.
35. **Leftover placeholders.** [Your Name], [Insert statistic], 2025-XX-XX. Instead, fill in from context, or flag clearly for Kirsty.

## Markup and citations

36. **Markdown where it doesn't render** (## or ** in an email, a Word doc, a web form). Instead, plain text.
37. **Chatbot code left in text or links.** ChatGPT: "contentReference," "oaicite," "oai_citation," "turn0search0," "attributableIndex," "+1." Gemini: "[cite: 1]," "[span_1](start_span)." Grok: "grok_card." DeepSeek: 【lenticular brackets】 and † daggers. Perplexity: "attached_file," "ppl-ai-file-upload." Also ":::writing" and "utm_source=chatgpt.com" in URLs. Instead, delete and clean the URL.
38. **Made-up or broken citations.** Fake or mismatched DOIs and ISBNs, dead links, book citations with no page numbers, real sources that don't support the claim. Instead, never invent a source. If a citation looks off, flag it.

## Other giveaways

39. **Sudden shift in voice** partway through, or a draft that sounds nothing like Kirsty's other writing. Instead, keep one voice, hers.
40. **Wrong variety of English.** AI defaults to American English. Instead, match whatever spelling Kirsty's original uses (American or Australian/British) and keep it consistent.
41. **Abrupt cut-offs** mid-thought. Instead, finish the thought or cut the fragment.
42. **Long, polished notes about small edits.** Instead, keep notes about changes short.

## What the article says human writing has more of

Use these on purpose:

- Simple "there is," "it has," "is," "are."
- Plain verbs: wrote, moved, used, tried.
- Definite statements when they're true: "was the first," "is the only," "one of the largest."
- Ordinary qualifiers and intensifiers: very, perhaps, tends to, pretty, a bit.

## Final check before handing it back

- Search for "—". There should be none.
- Search for the words in item 10. Each one should be gone or have a real, literal reason to stay.
- Look for "not just," "rather than," "not X but Y." Rewrite them as straight statements.
- Count list items. Any group of exactly three? Make sure it's really three things.
- Look at sentence lengths and punctuation. If every sentence is medium length and uses only commas and periods, vary it.
- Look at the first and last sentences. Neither should be a chatbot line or a summary.
- Make sure every fact in the original is still there and nothing new was invented.
