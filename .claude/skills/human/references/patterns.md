# Patterns to remove

Source: Wikipedia, "Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing). Kirsty reviewed and confirmed this list. Each entry says what the pattern is and what to do instead.

One caveat from the article itself: none of these alone proves a text is AI-written, and perfect grammar or a plain tone isn't a sign. The goal here isn't to make text messy. It's to make it sound like a person.

## Content

1. **Inflated significance or legacy.** "Stands as a testament," "plays a vital/pivotal role," "marks a turning point," "reflects broader trends," "leaves an indelible mark," "deeply rooted," "the evolving landscape." Instead, state the concrete fact and let the reader judge its importance.
2. **Overselling notability or press.** "Has been featured in numerous outlets," "maintains an active social media presence." Instead, name the outlet and what it said, or cut it.
3. **Tacked-on "-ing" analysis.** Sentences ending in "highlighting its importance," "ensuring continuity," "reflecting its heritage," "contributing to," "fostering," "showcasing," "emphasizing." Instead, end the sentence at the fact, or make the effect its own sentence with a real subject.
4. **Promotional puffery.** "Boasts," "vibrant," "rich cultural heritage," "nestled," "in the heart of," "breathtaking," "stunning," "renowned," "groundbreaking," "world-class," "commitment to excellence." Instead, describe what's there.
5. **Lecturing asides.** "It's important to note," "It is worth mentioning," "It's crucial to remember." Instead, just say the thing.
6. **Summary wrap-ups.** "In summary," "In conclusion," "Overall," followed by a restatement. Instead, end on the last real point.
7. **Stock "challenges and future prospects."** "Despite its successes, X faces several challenges..." then a hopeful closing. Instead, name the actual challenge and what's being done, or leave it out.
8. **Vague attribution (weasel words).** "Experts argue," "Observers note," "Industry reports suggest," "Some critics say." Instead, name the source or drop the claim.

## Word choice and grammar

9. **AI vocabulary.** Additionally (especially to open a sentence), align with, bolster, crucial, delve, emphasize, enduring, enhance, foster, garner, highlight (as a verb), interplay, intricate/intricacies, key (as an adjective), landscape (abstract), meticulous, pivotal, showcase, tapestry, testament, underscore, valuable, vibrant. Also watch: seamless, robust, leverage, holistic, empower, navigate (abstract), realm, multifaceted, transformative, game-changer, unlock, elevate, streamline, synergy. Instead, use the plain word: also, important, help, show, use, has.
10. **Avoiding "is" and "has."** "Serves as," "stands as," "represents," "features," "offers," "boasts" where "is" or "has" works. Instead, use "is" or "has."
11. **Negative parallelisms.** "Not only X, but also Y," "It's not just X, it's Y," "Not X, but Y," "This isn't about X. It's about Y." Instead, state Y directly.
12. **Rule of three.** Everything in triplets: three adjectives, three benefits, three examples. Instead, use the number of items you really have. One strong one often beats three.
13. **Elegant variation.** Swapping synonyms for the same thing each time (the organization, the group, the entity, the firm). Instead, pick one name and repeat it.
14. **False ranges.** "From X to Y" where X and Y don't sit on a real scale ("from grassroots advocacy to cutting-edge technology"). Instead, list the actual things or pick one.

## Style and formatting

15. **Title Case In Headings.** Instead, use sentence case.
16. **Too much bold.** Instead, bold almost nothing. Maybe one key term in a long document.
17. **Bold-label bullets.** Bullets that open with a bolded label and a colon ("**Scalability:** ..."). Instead, write a sentence, or plain bullets without labels. (This reference file uses them only because it's an internal checklist, not output.)
18. **Emoji** in headings, bullets, or professional text. Instead, none, unless Kirsty's original had them on purpose (like a casual social post).
19. **Em dashes (—).** Instead, use a comma, period, colon, or parentheses. Don't swap in a spaced hyphen ( - ) as a disguised dash either.
20. **Curly quotes and apostrophes** mixed into plain text. Instead, use straight ' and ".
21. **Unnecessary tables.** Instead, use a sentence or two unless the data really is a grid.
22. **Letter or email framing where it doesn't belong.** Salutations, subject lines, sign-offs on something that isn't a letter. Instead, start with the content.
23. **Hyphens for ranges.** "2019-2024," "pages 10-15." Instead, use an en dash (2019–2024) or "to" (2019 to 2024).

## Chatbot leftovers

24. **Helper lines.** "I hope this helps," "Certainly!", "Of course!", "Here's a...", "Let me know if you'd like...", "Would you like me to...", "Feel free to reach out." Instead, delete. If an email needs a closing line, make it specific ("Can you send the budget by Friday?").
25. **Knowledge-cutoff hedges.** "As of my last update," "While specific details are limited," "Based on available information." Instead, delete, or replace with the actual date or source.
26. **AI self-reference or refusals.** "As an AI language model..." Instead, delete.
27. **Leftover placeholders.** [Your Name], [Insert statistic], [Company], 2025-XX-XX. Instead, fill in from context, or flag them clearly for Kirsty.
28. **Flattery.** "Great question!", "You're absolutely right!", "What a fantastic idea!" Instead, delete.

## Markup and citations

29. **Markdown in places that don't render it** (## or ** in an email body, a Word doc, a web form). Instead, plain text.
30. **Chatbot code in links or text.** "turn0search0," "contentReference," "oaicite," "utm_source=chatgpt.com." Instead, delete and clean the URL.
31. **Made-up or broken citations.** Fake DOIs or ISBNs, dead links, real sources that don't support the claim. Instead, never invent a source. If a citation looks off, flag it for Kirsty.

## Other giveaways

32. **Abrupt cut-offs** mid-thought. Instead, finish the thought or cut the fragment.
33. **Sudden shifts in voice** partway through (casual paragraph, then corporate paragraph). Instead, keep one consistent voice, ideally Kirsty's.
34. **Long, polished explanations of small changes.** Instead, keep notes about edits short.

## Final check before handing it back

- Search the text for "—". There should be none.
- Search for the words in item 9. Each one should be gone or have a real reason to stay.
- Count list items. Any group of exactly three? Make sure it's really three things.
- Look at the first and last sentence. Neither should be a chatbot line or a summary.
- Make sure every fact in the original is still there and nothing new was invented.
