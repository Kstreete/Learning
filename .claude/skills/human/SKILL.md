# Human

Kirsty wants writing that sounds like a person wrote it. She has already done the homework on what gives AI text away (the Wikipedia "Signs of AI writing" guide), and she does not want to explain it again. This skill holds all of that so every rewrite comes out clean.

## Who you're writing for

Kirsty Streete is a business developer and fundraising consultant (her own firm is The Aussie Group LLC). She builds new business for The Nash Group, including AGI Affinity, its AI-readiness and digital-equity consulting arm, and she oversees several Nash Group affiliates, including affordable housing development and consulting. She is also a part-time UMGC student.

What that means for the writing:

- Her natural register is a confident, warm, direct business developer. Sound like someone who closes deals and talks to funders, not like a brochure.
- She is not a housing-finance technician. If a draft leans on LIHTC, tax credit, or deal-structure jargon, keep the terms the reader needs but say what they mean in plain words. Don't make her sound more technical than she is.
- For UMGC coursework, keep the academic register the assignment needs (citations, thesis, formal structure) and just remove the AI tells. Formal is fine. Robotic is not.

## How to do it

1. Read the whole draft first and work out what it is actually trying to say, who will read it, and what the reader should do next.
2. Rewrite it. Keep every real fact, name, number, date, and commitment. Never invent details, quotes, statistics, or sources to make the text feel more concrete. If something is vague and you can't fix it without facts you don't have, leave a short bracketed question for Kirsty, like [which year?].
3. Check the rewrite against the pattern list in `references/patterns.md`. Read that file every time. It's the full list and it's what Kirsty signed off on.
4. Read the result out loud in your head. If a sentence sounds like nobody would say it across a table, rewrite it.
5. Give her back the rewritten text, ready to paste. After it, add at most two or three short lines on what you changed, only if the changes were big. Skip the note for small edits. No "I hope this helps," no offers of more versions.

## What human writing looks like

These are the habits to reach for. The patterns file covers what to avoid.

- Plain verbs. "Is," "has," "runs," "built," "needs." Not "serves as," "boasts," "stands as."
- Specifics over importance claims. Instead of saying something is pivotal, say what it did: how many homes, which county, what changed.
- Varied sentence length. Some short ones. Some that run on a bit because the thought needs room.
- Lists only when the content really is a list. Two items or four items are fine. Don't force three.
- Commas, periods, parentheses, and colons instead of em dashes.
- Contractions where a person would use them (it's, we're, don't), unless the piece is formal academic writing.
- Straight quotes and apostrophes.
- Let the last sentence carry real information. Don't end on a summary or an inspirational line.
- Name who said or found something. If you can't name them, drop the claim or ask Kirsty.

## Quick example

Before:
> AGI Affinity stands as a testament to The Nash Group's commitment to digital equity — not just providing training, but fostering lasting transformation. Our holistic approach empowers communities, enhances workforce readiness, and drives innovation across the evolving landscape of AI.

After:
> AGI Affinity is The Nash Group's AI-readiness practice. We help community organizations and small employers get their teams comfortable with AI tools, and we focus on the groups that usually get left out of that training.

The second version is shorter, makes a concrete claim, and has no em dash, no triplet, no "not just X but Y," and no AI vocabulary.

## When it's Claude's own writing

If Kirsty has asked for human writing and you're drafting something new for her (an email, a summary, a reply), follow all of the above from the start rather than writing first and cleaning up after. The same goes for chat replies to her: no chatbot openers or closers, no bold-label bullet lists unless she asks for that format.
